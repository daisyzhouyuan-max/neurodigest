1Redox-dependent remodeling of mitochondrial nucleoids drives bioenergetic decline in aging neurons

Neuron  Xiu-Tang Cheng & Zu-Hang Sheng
Schematic drawn from the reported findings — not a figure from the paper.
Why it matters
Identifies a tractable mechanism for the bioenergetic failure of aging neurons: age-associated oxidative stress remodels mitochondrial nucleoids from compact, transcriptionally active structures into decompacted forms, lowering mtDNA transcript abundance and ATP output. The defect is reversible — expressing an oxidation-resistant TFAM variant restored nucleoid organization, transcript levels and ATP production in aged neurons, nominating nucleoid architecture itself as a target. The redox chemistry is species-specific: cysteine oxidation promotes human TFAM polymerization, whereas methionine oxidation weakens mouse TFAM–mtDNA binding.
Model & method
Aged mouse neurons and human iPSC-derived neurons; MINFLUX nanoscopy with single-molecule transcription mapping; oxidation-resistant TFAM rescue.
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2026.10.003

2Cell-specific transcriptional dysregulation in HD-positive human fetal striatum

Brain  Sophie V Precious & Anne E Rosser
Schematic drawn from the reported findings — not a figure from the paper.
Why it matters
Provides direct molecular evidence that Huntington's disease pathology is established during prenatal striatal development, decades before clinical onset. Single-cell RNA-seq of an HD-positive human fetal striatal sample (CAG 49) against an age- and sex-matched control identified 2,032 differentially expressed genes across nine cell clusters, with substantial — though incomplete — overlap with postmortem adult HD striatal signatures, and some changes reversed in direction. The finding reframes HD as having an early neurodevelopmental component rather than a purely degenerative one. The comparison rests on a single HD fetal sample versus one control, so cluster-level signatures require replication.
Model & method
Human fetal striatal tissue (HD-positive, CAG 16/49, vs control 16/17); single-cell RNA-seq; comparison with public adult postmortem HD datasets.
https://doi.org/10.1093/brain/awag374

3Filamin-A links immediate-early-gene transcriptional disruption to dopaminergic decline in PSP

Brain  Koyo Tsujikawa & Masahisa Katsuno
Schematic drawn from the reported findings — not a figure from the paper.
Why it matters
Connects cytoskeletal stress to the dopaminergic failure of progressive supranuclear palsy. In mice overexpressing human filamin-A, immediate-early-gene-centred transcriptional dysregulation with chromatin-accessibility changes activated AP-1 gene programmes and produced cortical monoamine depletion alongside early striatal dopaminergic sensitization; with ageing this coupling was lost, giving way to dopaminergic exhaustion and locomotor, cognitive and social deficits. PSP postmortem cortex and striatum (11 patients vs 5 controls) showed reduced dopamine D2 receptor expression and AP-1 motif enrichment in regions of reduced chromatin accessibility, supporting human relevance of the mouse mechanism.
Model & method
Human filamin-A-overexpressing mice (young 5–7 mo, aged 14–16 mo), multi-omics (transcriptomic/epigenomic/metabolomic/behavioural) across brain regions; human PSP postmortem biochemical validation.
https://doi.org/10.1093/brain/awag228

The hypothalamus as a central hub for metabolic flexibility and brain health

Neuron  Tamas L Horvath & Daniela Cota
Key findings
A perspective arguing that organism-wide metabolic flexibility is coordinated by the hypothalamus, with AgRP and POMC melanocortin neurons integrating metabolic, hormonal and immune signals to shape reward, learning, executive function and social behaviour while imposing metabolic states on peripheral tissues. The authors propose that loss of this flexibility — with aging or overnutrition — renders body–brain communication rigid and drives cognitive decline, chronic inflammation and neurodegenerative disease, framing peripheral signals as determinants rather than modifiers of brain function. Conceptual synthesis rather than new data.
Model & method
Review/perspective; melanocortin (AgRP/POMC) circuits as the organizing model.
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2026.09.028
Neurodegeneration & aging

A non-invasive vasomechanic intervention increases efflux of brain-derived proteins in humans

Nat Commun  Johanna Tuunanen & Vesa Kiviniemi
Key findings
In 29 healthy adults, combining sublingual nitric oxide with Piezo1-targeted whole-body vibration was associated with correlated changes in brain-fluid dynamics and increased blood levels of brain-derived proteins (Aβ40/42, GFAP), consistent with transient redistribution of brain solutes toward the circulation. Effects scaled with age and with the magnitude of NO-induced hypotension, and the pre-specified primary endpoints (circulating brain-protein concentrations, brain-fluid imaging, brain pulsation) were met. An early, small proof-of-concept in healthy volunteers rather than patients, so clinical relevance to neurodegeneration is unestablished.
Model & method
Healthy adults (n=29); multimodal neuroimaging plus blood biomarkers; sublingual NO + Piezo1-targeted whole-body vibration.
https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-026-78894-y

Hepatocyte exosomal miR-122 mediates liver-to-brain neuroinflammation in alcohol-exposed AD mice

Proc Natl Acad Sci U S A  Veronika Brezani & Gyongyi Szabo
Key findings
In APP/PS1 mice, repeated binge alcohol produced liver injury (elevated serum ALT) and raised pro-inflammatory cytokines in both liver and brain, and increased hepatocyte release of small extracellular vesicles enriched in miR-122-5p. miR-122-5p was detected in the brain of alcohol-exposed AD mice and taken up by microglia; it sensitized microglia to LPS (increased CCL2, CXCL2, CCL3, TNF-α), a miR-122-5p mimic partially reproduced this sensitization and an inhibitor reversed it, while the target ADAM10 fell in brain after alcohol. The data support an EV-borne liver-to-brain axis amplifying neuroinflammation; evidence is mouse and in vitro.
Model & method
APP/PS1 AD mice with repeated binge alcohol; hepatocyte-derived sEVs; BV-2 microglia; miR-122-5p mimic/inhibitor.
https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2535191123

A protocol to isolate and lipidomically profile neuronal lipid droplets

Nat Protoc  Mukesh Kumar & Timothy A Ryan
Key findings
A method addressing a long-standing obstacle — the submicron size and low abundance of neuronal lipid droplets, whose existence was itself debated. Blocking neuron-specific triglyceride lipase enlarges droplets enough for flotation on a sucrose gradient, after which LC-MS lipidomics resolves their composition from cultured primary neurons over a 3–4 week workflow. The approach opens neuronal lipid-droplet composition to disease-specific analysis relevant to neurodegeneration. A methods protocol rather than a biological finding.
Model & method
Cultured primary neurons; triglyceride-lipase inhibition and sucrose-gradient flotation; LC-MS lipidomics.
https://doi.org/10.1038/s41596-026-01451-w

Imipramine binds amyloid-β monomers and is associated with lower mortality in AD patients

bioRxiv  Johnson, N. R. & Potter, H.
Key findings
The tricyclic imipramine binds Aβ monomers directly (by NMR), inhibiting the apoE–Aβ interaction, and reduced Aβ accumulation and neuronal loss in hiPSC-derived AD cerebral organoids. Imipramine-treated female transgenic AD rats showed improved reference memory with increased hippocampal cellularity and cholinergic markers. In the National Alzheimer's Coordinating Center database, AD patients prescribed imipramine had lower 10-year all-cause mortality than those on other antidepressants or none. The human mortality signal is retrospective and observational, so confounding by indication cannot be excluded; the authors propose prospective evaluation, particularly in APOE4 carriers.
Model & method
NMR of imipramine–Aβ binding; hiPSC AD cerebral organoids; transgenic AD rats (Morris water maze); retrospective NACC mortality analysis (Kaplan–Meier, Cox).
https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.10.28.762584

Oligodendrocytes may supply the extra density that defines MSA α-synuclein filaments

bioRxiv  Zhang, D. & Scheres, S.
Key findings
MSA brain-derived α-synuclein seeds were used to template aggregation in SH-SY5Y cells, human iNeurons, mouse cortical cultures and oligodendrocyte-enriched cultures (all overexpressing human α-synuclein), with cryo-EM of the seeded filaments. Authentic MSA filaments carry two protofilaments with an unidentified non-proteinaceous density between them; all seeded filaments formed a single protofilament, but only those seeded in oligodendrocytes reproduced the extra density. Striatal injection of MSA seeds into mice expressing human α-synuclein under the oligodendrocyte-specific PLP promoter likewise yielded the extra density, suggesting oligodendrocytes provide the chemical component underlying MSA filament structure.
Model & method
Seeded aggregation across cell types with cryo-EM structure determination; PLP-promoter human α-synuclein transgenic mice.
https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.10.29.762735
Preprint · not peer reviewed

Donor-aware reanalysis supports STMN2 depletion with nuclear TDP-43 loss in FTD and ALS

bioRxiv  Chaudhary, A. & Yao, S.
Key findings
A reanalysis of public RNA-seq from FACS-isolated NeuN-positive frontal-cortex nuclei (GSE126542; 7 donors, 5 matched pairs), modelling donor structure explicitly, found STMN2 robustly depleted in nuclear-TDP-43-loss neurons (log2 fold change −1.81, FDR 6.2e-15; ~71.5% lower), consistent across all five donor pairs, with TARDBP also reduced. Whole-gene UNC13A expression was modestly higher and significant only in the donor-aware model, and — the authors emphasize — cannot be used to infer cryptic-exon inclusion or functional UNC13A loss. A purely computational reanalysis without splice-junction, isoform or protein data, so mechanistic claims await junction-level and experimental validation.
Model & method
Donor-aware PyDESeq2 reanalysis of human neuronal-nuclei RNA-seq.
https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.10.27.762172

Inflammatory activation creates a lysosomal bottleneck for microglial aggregate clearance

bioRxiv  Eikens, F. & Scheiblich, H.
Key findings
LPS/TLR4 inflammatory activation impaired microglial degradation of internalized α-synuclein and tau aggregates while largely preserving their uptake. Mechanistically, TLR4 drove translational reprogramming and raised intracellular proteostatic demand; inhibiting protein synthesis restored α-synuclein degradation without blocking NF-κB activation. Canonical autophagic flux was largely preserved, but lysosomal proteins including cathepsin D were reduced, and pharmacological TFEB activation restored aggregate degradation — defining a demand-driven lysosomal bottleneck. In dementia with Lewy bodies tissue, microglial α-synuclein deposits showed limited spatial association with cathepsin D-positive structures.
Model & method
LPS-activated microglia loaded with α-synuclein/tau aggregates; protein-synthesis inhibition and TFEB activation; DLB postmortem tissue.
https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.11.02.763615

Brain mitovesicles are remodeled stage-specifically during amyloid-β pathology

bioRxiv  Voorbraeck, L. & Naia, L.
Key findings
Forebrain-derived mitovesicles — a mitochondrial-origin extracellular-vesicle subpopulation — were isolated from App knock-in AD mice at presymptomatic (2 mo) and symptomatic (12 mo) stages by density-gradient fractionation. Their predominantly inner-membrane proteome was remodeled in a stage-specific way: presymptomatic AppKI mitovesicles showed reduced fatty-acid β-oxidation and lipid-metabolism proteins before any respiratory deficit, whereas symptomatic mitovesicles were enriched for oxidative-phosphorylation, membrane and transporter proteins yet showed reduced Complex IV-linked respiration — a dissociation between cargo abundance and function. A conserved 62-protein mitovesicle core and a 25-protein disease signature were shared with human AD cortex and hippocampus.
Model & method
Density-gradient isolation of mitovesicles from App knock-in mice and human AD tissue; quantitative proteomics with Seahorse respirometry.
https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.10.30.762534
